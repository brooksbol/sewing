# Seam constructions and edge finishing

Reusable techniques observed or considered in actual garments. Techniques are not automatic instructions for every project.

## Plain structural seams + symmetrical topstitching

**Proven on a previously made denim shirt; proposed for barn coat.**
- Use sound structural stitching first; heavy topstitching is decorative/reinforcing, not a replacement.
- Press seam allowances open.
- Place a decorative row approximately **1/4 in to either side** of the seam, yielding roughly **1/2 in** between rows.
- Motivated by a symmetrical look, particularly at split back, long sleeve seams, and side seams.

## Folded-edge directional topstitching

**Proven on shirt cuff/yoke junction; considered for barn coat facings/edges.**
- First decorative line approximately **1 mm** from the fold.
- Second line another **1/4 in** from the first.
- Prior decorative thread: Gütermann Denim top thread, thinner commercial polyester in bobbin; around **4 mm stitch length** on the shirt. Coat medium-brown thread selected.

## Turned self-fabric facing / hem

**Experimental on barn coat canvas, not finalized.** Sew a matching canvas facing right sides together at the edge, turn to interior, create an attachment boundary for subsequent lining. Similar in concept to making shirt cuffs. Corners, grading/bulk, lining ease, and order of construction still being worked out.

## Enclosed shell seams

On a planned fully lined coat, hidden plain shell seam allowances need not all be flat-felled or overlocked. The maker currently does neither on barn coat canvas. This is a garment-specific choice, **not** evidence that every concealed seam on every fabric can safely remain untreated. Inspect fraying and grade at bulky intersections.

## Notes for future entries

For each seam construction, distinguish its structural stitch, seam allowance, pressing, visible topstitch pattern, fabric constraints, and whether the maker has actually tested it. Keep a link to the relevant garment project.
